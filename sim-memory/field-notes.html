Field Notes: The harness is not the memory — Self-Improvement Memory and the optimizer's notebook

Series framing. This is a Field Notes piece, not a numbered teardown. Every teardown in this series dissects a system that runs an agent. The subject here runs nothing the reader can install: it is a paper — Yu et al., Self-Improvement Memory Enables Continual Recursive Self-Improvement (Google Cloud AI, Google DeepMind, Penn State) — and it releases no code. What it offers instead is the first controlled evidence on a question two earlier pieces in this series approached from opposite sides. The Darwin Gödel Machine teardown (#13) examined a harness that rewrites itself and keeps an archive of its variants, but no structured account of why its edits worked. The Hy Memory Field Notes examined memory as a write-path engineering problem inside a harness. This paper moves the memory one level up: not the agent's memory of its tasks, but the optimizer's memory of its own edits — and tests whether that memory, rather than the evolved harness itself, is the asset that transfers when the model or the benchmark changes. That is a Field Notes question, and this piece answers it from the paper's own evidence, graded as paper evidence throughout: nothing here was verified against source code, because there is none to verify against.

The problem: an evolved harness does not explain itself

Harness optimization — an outer loop in which a proposer suggests edits to an agent's harness, an evaluator scores them, and a selector keeps the winners — is usually studied with the policy model and the benchmark held fixed. The paper names the setting where that stops being true Continual Recursive Self-Improvement (CRSI): optimization continues after the benchmark changes (cross-dataset CRSI, policy fixed) or after the policy model changes (cross-model CRSI, benchmark fixed), starting from the previously evolved harness instead of from scratch.1

The motivating observation is that evolution's gains do not reliably survive the move, and the evolved artifact cannot say why. Source optimization raised held-out source scores by 2.75–6.41 points in all six cross-dataset settings — yet the source-evolved FewShotAll harness starts target optimization at 72.92, below the un-evolved seed's 75.72.2 In the cross-model experiments, an Opus-evolved harness first run on Gemini scores below Gemini's own seed harness in every domain, by 0.60 to 5.48 points; in the workspace domain it starts at 68.41 against the seed's 73.89.3 Continuing to optimize from that harness does not reliably fix it: harness-only continuation finishes below a cold start in three of the four cross-model domains.3 The paper's diagnosis is that the harness alone leaves three questions unanswered — why was an edit proposed, what was it expected to fix, and what happened when it was tested? Source optimization produces exactly this evidence. It just does not normally travel with the harness.1

The mechanism: a notebook keyed by problem, not by time

Self-Improvement Memory (SIM) is that evidence, structured. An entry is keyed by a root cause — a recurring harness problem, such as premature completion — and holds a sequence of attempts against it. Each attempt records the modification and its rationale, the predicted outcome (which tasks the edit is expected to fix, written down before evaluation) and the observed outcome, and provenance: domain, benchmark, policy model, and round.4 Two properties of the record matter more than its schema:

  • Failures are kept. Each round, only the selected candidate is recorded — including when the edit regresses or fails to fix the tasks it predicted it would. Entries whose root-cause text matches are merged without dropping their attempt histories, and an entry survives the replacement of the very edit it describes.45
  • There is no retrieval. The proposer receives the complete SIM in its system prompt every round, and the selector reads the same record when screening candidates. Nothing is ranked, truncated, or forgotten by a retriever; both LLM "harness engineers" judge each inherited edit against the current failures and may retain, revise, replace, or remove the modification while keeping its record.46

The experiment then varies only what crosses the boundary. Four conditions start target optimization: Cold Start (seed harness, no memory), Harness Transfer (evolved harness, no memory), Problem-Only Memory (evolved harness plus the root causes alone), and Full SIM (evolved harness plus the complete record). Starting harness, target tasks, policy, round counts (10), and candidate budgets (5 per round) are matched across conditions.6

The evidence

Cross-dataset (six transitions across coding, text classification, workspace, and engineering seeds): Full SIM finishes highest in all six, beating Harness Transfer by 2.43–4.35 points and Problem-Only Memory by 0.20–6.06. The ablation is the interesting part: Problem-Only Memory is unreliable, ranging from 1.82 points below Harness Transfer to 2.40 above it. The diagnosis alone is not the asset; the asset is the diagnosis with its attempts and outcomes attached.2

Cross-model (Opus 5.5 → Gemini 3.8 Flash, benchmark fixed): Full SIM finishes highest in all four domains, beating harness-only continuation by 3.60–11.33 points. The workspace case is the headline: both conditions start at 68.41; Harness Transfer reaches 73.42, Full SIM reaches 84.75.3 Across model generations (a Gemini 2.5 Flash → 3.5 Flash → 3.8 Flash → 4 Argon chain on Terminal-Bench 2.1, carrying harness and SIM forward), continuation beats fresh per-generation optimization in two of three subsequent generations — and loses on the fourth, 94.8 to 95.6.7

The load-bearing result is the paired study. Ten independent Terminus-2 source runs, each continued on Terminal-Bench 4 with and without its SIM under matched budgets: SIM finishes higher in 9 of 10 pairs, mean advantage +2.36 points (95% CI [1.05, 3.68]; paired t(9) = 4.06, p = 0.003). One pair favors no-SIM continuation, by 0.61.8 Everything else in the paper is, by the authors' own reporting convention, one complete run per condition — descriptive, not a variance estimate. The paired study is the piece of evidence that survives that caveat.

The mechanism shows up in the cases. Both coding conditions inherit the same completion gate from source optimization. With SIM, the proposer reads the gate's rationale and source outcomes, retains completion verification, and extends it with target-specific checks — feasibility versus optimality. Without SIM, the run tightens the inherited gate, twice repairs completion deadlocks of its own making, and misattributes the gate to its own first round. The requirement transferred; only the memory condition determined whether its implementation was revised or thrashed.5 Retention tells the same story from the other side: Full SIM loses 3 of 225 previously successful source rollouts during target optimization, against 6 for harness-only continuation.5

The second lens: the survey wrote the contract first

The companion survey — same group, revised one day after this paper appeared — never names SIM, but its appendix on persistent improvement evidence prescribes, almost clause for clause, the record SIM implements: a memory entry should connect "the initiating failure, attempted edit, positive and negative evaluation evidence, and the conditions under which reuse is warranted."9 Its comparison table likewise demands the controls this paper mostly runs: retained versus reset improvement state at matched budgets, with initialization and subsequent improvement reported separately.10 And its conclusion draws the distinction the whole result hangs on: improving an agent is not the same as improving how later agents are improved; a retained artifact earns its claim only when it demonstrably changes later decisions.11 Read together, the two papers are a prescription and its first controlled test — published together, whether or not they were written that way.

Limits, on the record

  • Paper evidence only. No code or checkpoints were released; the proposer/selector prompts exist as appendix summaries, and no claim in this piece could be checked against an implementation. That is a weaker footing than any numbered teardown in this series, and the reason this is a Field Note.
  • One run per condition outside coding — the authors state this themselves: independent repetitions were affordable only in the coding domain.12 The cross-model headline (84.75) is a single run.
  • The engineers are models too. Proposer and selector are LLMs (Gemini 3.8 Flash in the main comparisons); an ablation with Claude Opus 5.5 in those roles produced larger SIM gains from both coding seeds — so the size of the effect depends on who is doing the remembering and revising.6
  • Whole-memory prompting does not scale by construction. Reading the entire record every round is a deliberate design choice at this history length; the paper does not test where it breaks.
  • No cost accounting across conditions. The procedure is evaluated whole — inherited memory plus continued updating — and the paper reports no token or runtime comparison between memory conditions, the very joint reporting its companion survey recommends.1012

What this changes

Every harness in this series keeps some account of itself: transcripts, event logs, skill libraries, archives of variants. The consistent finding across the teardowns — stated as F2 in the survey paper this site accompanies — is that what a system stores is usually a transcript, and what it can use is knowledge distilled from one. SIM is that distinction applied to the one actor the teardowns never instrument: the engineer doing the editing. Its result, at the strength the paired study supports, is that when a harness must outlive its original model and benchmark, the transferable asset is not the evolved artifact. It is the notebook — problems, attempts, predictions, outcomes — that explains how the artifact came to be.
